> **The short version:** Starlink Mobile, SpaceX's push to become a fourth national wireless carrier, has set off a Washington fight. AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile are lobbying the FCC to scale back a plan that would let satellites use more of their spectrum, just as SpaceX reportedly seeks access to 80,000 U.S. cell sites. The FCC is expected to take up the proposal on October 29.

Starlink Mobile has gone from a satellite add-on to a direct threat to the three biggest U.S. phone companies in a matter of weeks. SpaceX has been buying spectrum, winning FCC approvals and, according to Bloomberg, negotiating for space on tens of thousands of cell towers. Now the incumbents are pushing back at the regulator. Here is what is happening, who wants what, and what it could mean for the phone in your pocket.

## What Starlink Mobile is trying to become

SpaceX's satellite-to-phone service started as a way to send texts from places with no cell coverage. The ambition is now much larger. According to Tech Insider's summary of SpaceX statements, the company describes its planned network as combining its satellite-to-mobile constellation with "an advanced terrestrial deployment," and says the spectrum it is acquiring will help it become "a major mobile carrier in the U.S."

Elon Musk has described a low-band spectrum purchase as "the last critical piece of the spectrum puzzle needed for SpaceX to provide complete phone coverage in America," as quoted by Tech Insider.

### The building blocks so far

| Piece | What has been reported |
| --- | --- |
| Satellites | FCC approval for 15,000 next-generation satellites tuned for direct-to-cell service |
| Spectrum | 2 GHz airwaves bought from EchoStar, plus nationwide low-band spectrum; FCC approval of Tristar's AWS-3 spectrum |
| Towers | Talks for access to at least 80,000 U.S. cell sites |

## How satellite-to-phone service works

Direct-to-cell satellites act like cell towers in orbit. Instead of needing a special satellite phone, an ordinary smartphone connects to a satellite using the same radio frequencies it already uses to talk to a nearby tower. That is why spectrum is the heart of this fight: the satellites have to borrow, lease or own frequencies that phones already support.

Under the FCC's Supplemental Coverage from Space framework, satellite operators partner with carriers that hold those licenses. The more bands that qualify, the more capacity a satellite network can offer, and the less it depends on any one carrier partner. That is good for a newcomer like SpaceX, and it is exactly what worries the incumbents, who paid billions at auction for the same airwaves and fear interference with ground networks that carry most of the country's mobile traffic.

## The 80,000 tower talks

On October 22, Bloomberg reported that SpaceX is in talks with cellular infrastructure companies to secure access to at least 80,000 U.S. sites, citing people familiar with the matter, according to [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/spacex-seeks-access-80000-us-cell-sites-wireless-network-bloomberg-news-reports-2026-10-22/).

Key details:

- **Scale:** 80,000 sites would equal about half of the 158,500 purpose-built cell towers operating in the U.S. last year, according to the Wireless Infrastructure Association, as cited by Reuters.
- **Who owns the towers:** Crown Castle operates about 40,000 U.S. towers, American Tower about 42,000, and privately held Vertical Bridge about 18,000, per the Reuters report carried by the Star-Advertiser.
- **What Crown Castle said:** Asked about potential deals with SpaceX and Amazon on an earnings call, CEO Chris Hillabrant said, "I don't think I want to get into customer-specific detail, but you can assume that we're talking to all parties," [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/science/1017477/spacex-cell-sites-major-us-carrier) reports.

None of the companies has announced a deal. Reuters said Crown Castle, American Tower, Vertical Bridge and SpaceX did not immediately respond to requests for comment.

## The FCC plan the carriers are fighting

The second front is regulatory. On October 8, the FCC released a draft proposal to expand **Supplemental Coverage from Space (SCS)**, the framework that lets satellites connect directly to ordinary phones using frequencies licensed to mobile carriers.

According to Drive Tesla's review of the draft, the proposal would open another **482 megahertz** of spectrum and increase the number of eligible bands from **five to 13**.

### What AT&T, Verizon, T-Mobile and CTIA are asking for

In filings and meetings with the FCC, including an October 22 CTIA filing and October 20 meetings with advisers to Chairman Brendan Carr and Commissioners Anna Gomez and Olivia Trusty, the carriers and their trade group made these requests, according to [PhoneArena](https://www.phonearena.com/news/at-t-t-mobile-verizon-spacex-ctia_id184275) and Drive Tesla:

1. **Remove the TDD bands.** Drop Time-Division Duplex frequencies, including the 2.5 GHz and 3.45 GHz bands, from the plan. CTIA argues these are technically unsuitable for satellite service and support connectivity for more than 300 million users.
2. **Keep carrier control.** Preserve carriers' consent, control and termination rights, and keep existing spectrum leasing rules instead of new sharing agreements between satellite and ground operators.
3. **Tighter interference limits.** Revise the proposed aggregate power limit of -76 dBm/m²/MHz on satellite signals so ground networks on the same frequencies are protected.
4. **Equal performance rules.** Hold direct-to-device operators to performance requirements on par with carriers, and require ground construction from satellite operators that acquire new spectrum.

PhoneArena summarizes the carriers' position as framing satellite service as "secondary and complementary" to land networks, and warning against disrupting the "workhorse terrestrial 5G bands." PCMag reports that former FCC Chairman Ajit Pai also urged the commission to drop a wide swath of frequencies from the proposal.

## Why this matters for regular phone users

Most of this is happening in regulatory filings, but the outcome could reach your bill and your signal:

- **More competition.** A credible fourth national carrier could pressure prices and plan terms, especially in rural areas where satellites help most.
- **Better dead-zone coverage.** Expanding SCS could make satellite texting, and eventually data, more common on standard phones.
- **Interference risk.** The carriers argue that putting satellite signals on busy 5G bands could degrade service for existing customers. SpaceX and its supporters argue the FCC's limits are enough.
- **Market reaction.** BigGo Finance reported that AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile shares fell in premarket trading after the tower report, while tower owners Crown Castle and American Tower rose.

Analysts remain cautious. Yahoo Finance noted that "Starlink Mobile remains the larger long-term question given the size and profitability of the wireless market."

| Player | Main goal |
| --- | --- |

## What to watch next

- **October 29:** The FCC's open meeting, where the expanded SCS framework is expected to come up, according to Drive Tesla.
- **Tower deals:** Any signed lease with Crown Castle, American Tower or Vertical Bridge would be the clearest sign Starlink Mobile is building a ground network.
- **Pricing and launch details:** SpaceX has not announced consumer plans or prices.
- **Carrier responses:** Drive Tesla has reported on AT&T, T-Mobile and Verizon forming an alliance to respond to Starlink, so expect more joint filings and competing satellite offers.
