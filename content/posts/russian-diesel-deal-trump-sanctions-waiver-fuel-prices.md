> **The short version:** The Russian diesel deal lets Moscow send millions of barrels of diesel to the US and world markets, and on Friday President Trump also waived new Russia sanctions and tariffs that Congress passed last month. The White House says it is about peace talks and fuel prices. With diesel averaging $6.32 a gallon, analysts say the extra Russian supply is too small to move pump prices much, while Republicans and Ukraine are pushing back hard.

The Russian diesel deal has turned "diesel fuel" into one of the top searches in the US this week, and for good reason: it touches the price of nearly everything that moves by truck. In the space of a week, Trump announced that Vladimir Putin had agreed to release Russian diesel onto the global market, then signed an executive order waiving tough new sanctions that were days from taking effect. Here is what is actually in the deal, what the waiver does and does not do, what it means for prices at the pump, and why it has split Washington weeks before the November 3 midterm elections.

## What is in the Russian diesel deal

Trump first described the arrangement on Friday, October 9. According to a [Reuters explainer](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/what-is-diesel-deal-between-putin-trump-2026-10-12/), he said Russia would immediately supply more than 300,000 metric tons of diesel, or about 2.25 million barrels, to the US and global markets. He said another 500,000 tons would follow in November and a further 1 million tons "immediately thereafter."

Moscow moved the next day. Russia had banned diesel exports in July to protect its own supply after Ukrainian drone strikes on its refineries, and on Saturday, October 10, it partially lifted that ban with immediate effect. A wider ban on exports to countries without bilateral agreements was extended until October 31.

Reuters later summarized the planned flow as starting at about 2.3 million barrels this month and rising to about 7.6 million barrels a month. Here are the key numbers in one place:

| Item | Figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First shipment | More than 300,000 metric tons (about 2.25 million barrels) | Reuters |
| November volume | 500,000 tons | Reuters |
| Next tranche | 1 million tons "immediately thereafter" | Reuters |
| Planned monthly pace | About 7.6 million barrels | Reuters |
| Estimated value of first 300,000 tons | Roughly $400 million | Reuters |
| Russia's export capacity before drone strikes | About 100,000 tons a day | Reuters |
| US transport-sector diesel use | About 2.94 million barrels a day | EIA data via Reuters |

There are open practical questions. Reuters noted it remains unclear which tankers could carry the fuel, since many vessels that move Russian oil are themselves under sanctions. ABC News also pointed out that a 2022 US law explicitly bans the United States from importing Russian energy, which is part of why the deal drew immediate criticism.

## What the Russia sanctions waiver does

The second move came late on Friday, October 16. Trump signed an executive order waiving new sanctions on Russia and tariffs on the biggest buyers of Russian energy, two days before they were due to kick in, [ABC News reported](https://abcnews.com/US/energy-secretary-defends-waiving-new-sanctions-russia-economic/story?id=137359849).

Those penalties came from the bipartisan Graham Act, named for the late Republican Sen. Lindsey Graham of South Carolina. Trump signed it into law on September 18. Per Axios, it would have imposed new sanctions on Russia and tariffs on top buyers of Russian energy, a tool designed to squeeze Moscow's oil income.

Administration officials have stressed what the order does not do. On NBC's "Meet the Press," Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said, "No sanctions were lifted on Friday. No sanctions were lifted." He added, "There is a 60-day period so we can examine what's going on. The only thing that has been lifted is the diesel embargo," according to [The Hill](https://thehill.com/homenews/administration/6156573-bessent-refutes-sanctions-relief/). Bessent also noted that the UK waived its own embargo on diesel and jet fuel made from Russian crude in May.

Newsweek noted that the executive order itself does not mention the diesel deal by name, though it described the waiver and the diesel agreement as part of the administration's broader effort on energy prices.

### Mixed explanations from the White House

The administration has given more than one reason for the moves, as [Axios reported](https://www.axios.com/2026/10/18/trump-russia-diesel-deal-rubio-wright-sanctions):

- **Energy Secretary Chris Wright** framed it as leverage for peace: "This is a carrot to try to end a war. It's not an economic lifeline for Russia." He argued Russia only benefits if it can free up diesel to export, which means using less at home, scaling back the war or repairing refineries.
- **Secretary of State Marco Rubio** offered a different view: "That diesel was going to be sold one way or the other." He said Trump wants time to analyze what the sanctions and tariffs would mean for the global and US economy.
- **Trump** said on Sunday that "I am in the process of negotiating Peace in the war between Russia and Ukraine, and don't want to do anything that will drive, foolishly, Russia away from the bargaining table," [DW reported](https://www.dw.com/en/trump-defends-russia-sanctions-waivers-accuses-zelenskyy-of-prolonging-war/a-79694674).

## Diesel prices today

The reason any of this matters to ordinary drivers and businesses is the price of fuel. AAA's national averages on Sunday, October 18, show how far prices have climbed in a year:

| Fuel | Today | Week ago | Month ago | Year ago |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Diesel | $6.32 | $6.28 | $6.45 | $3.64 |
| Regular gasoline | $4.30 | $4.36 | $4.47 | $3.04 |

*Source: [AAA](https://gasprices.aaa.com/), figures rounded to the nearest cent.*

Diesel is up roughly 74% from a year ago, which feeds directly into trucking, farming and delivery costs. Fuel prices have risen during the US war with Iran, according to The Hill, and Reuters reported continuing attacks on oil tankers that have disrupted flows through the Strait of Hormuz. Gulf crude flows fell to a seven-day average of 8.5 million barrels a day last week, down from 20 million the week before, per Reuters.

## How oil markets reacted

Traders took the waiver as a sign that more Russian fuel could reach the market. On Monday, October 19, Brent crude fell $1.98, or 1.8%, to $107.88 a barrel by 04:27 GMT, after settling on Friday at its highest level since May 19, [Reuters reported](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/oil-slips-trumps-sanction-waiver-may-lead-more-russian-fuel-supplies-2026-10-18/). Brent had briefly touched $110 on Friday for the first time since May. US West Texas Intermediate slipped $1.16 to $100.25.

Analysts were skeptical that the drop would last:

- "Trump's move looks like another desperate attempt to keep oil prices in check ahead of the US midterm elections," said Priyanka Sachdeva of Phillip Nova, adding that it does little about the disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz.
- "Until the details and duration are clearer, the market is unlikely to fully price in a sustained increase in supply," said Tim Waterer of KCM Trade.
- Suvro Sarkar of DBS said the levers Trump has pulled, including the Russia waivers, "are short-term fixes and will have barely any structural impact on the supply situation." DBS expects oil to stay between $100 and $110 a barrel.

## Will the Russian diesel deal lower prices at the pump?

On the numbers, the effect looks small. Reuters calculated that the first 300,000 tons is less than one day's supply for the US transport sector alone, which uses about 2.94 million barrels of diesel a day. Even the November and follow-up tranches combined, 1.5 million tons, add up to less than four days of US transport demand. And the fuel is meant for global markets, not just the US.

DW reported that most economists believe the waiver and the diesel deal will do little to lower US fuel prices. The bigger drivers remain crude oil, which is still above $100 a barrel, and the shipping disruptions in the Gulf.

That does not mean zero impact. Extra supply at the margin can ease prices in tight markets, and Reuters reported that US prices fell 0.5% on the Monday after the first announcement. But AAA's figures show diesel actually ticked up over the past week, from $6.28 to $6.32, which suggests the deal alone has not changed the trend.

## Backlash from Republicans and Ukraine

The sanctions waiver has drawn open criticism from senior Republicans as well as from Kyiv. According to Axios:

- Sen. Thom Tillis (R-N.C.) called it "beyond irresponsible."
- Sen. John Cornyn (R-Texas) said the late Lindsey Graham "would be appalled."
- Sen. Ted Cruz (R-Texas) called it a "mistake."
- Rep. French Hill (R-Ark.) said the US should be increasing pressure on Russia and asked for a classified briefing.

Graham's sister, Darline Graham, told ABC News, "Personally, I would choose to keep the pressure on Russia to end the war and get the gas prices down, because I don't trust Putin and don't trust Russia." Earlier, Rep. Don Bacon said it was time to apply the sanctions Congress had passed, not lift them, per Reuters.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said of the waiver, "It definitely helps only the Russians," according to ABC News. He told reporters that "if the United States will take the side of Russia, of course we will not prevail," and accused Moscow of violating the energy ceasefire 76 times, per DW. Trump, for his part, accused Zelenskyy of not wanting to end the war.

Bessent pushed back on the idea that the US has switched sides, pointing to sanctions on Russian oil giants Rosneft and Lukoil imposed in 2025 and saying, "No one has been tougher on Russian sanctions than President Trump."

## What to watch next

- **The 60-day review.** Bessent and Rubio both pointed to a 60-day window to study the effect of the sanctions and tariffs. Whether the waiver is extended or the penalties snap back is the biggest open question.
- **Actual Russian shipments.** Watch for tanker tracking and Russian export data that show whether the promised 2.3 million barrels this month really leave port, and how sanctions on tankers affect delivery.
- **Russia's export ban deadline on October 31.** Moscow's broader diesel export ban runs until then; any extension or change will shape how much fuel can flow.
- **Weekly diesel prices.** AAA and EIA updates will show whether the national average moves meaningfully below $6.
- **Congress.** Republican senators are openly breaking with the White House, and lawmakers have requested briefings. Any move to override or tighten the waiver would be significant.
- **The midterms on November 3.** Fuel prices are a central campaign issue, so expect more energy announcements before Election Day.
