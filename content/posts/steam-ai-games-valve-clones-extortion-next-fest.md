> **The short version:** Valve says it will not ban or police which AI tools developers use, even as Steam AI games flood the store and indie studios report AI clone threats and extortion emails. Valve told developers in Busan that extortion is a matter for police, asked them to report it to Steam, and admitted that some low-quality games get exposure during Next Fest.

Steam AI games became one of the most searched gaming topics this week after Valve answered developers' questions in person about AI slop, copycat games and a new kind of shakedown email. The short answer from Valve: no new rules, no bans on tools, and an admission that the problem is real. Here is what Valve actually said, how big the AI wave on Steam has become, and what it means for players and developers.

## What Valve said about Steam AI games in Busan

The comments came at Steam Indie Connect, a developer event in Busan, South Korea, on October 27, 2026, where members of Valve's Steam Business Team, Tom Giardino and Marc Wilkinson, took questions from local developers, according to [Inven Global](https://www.invenglobal.com/articles/27761/valve-responds-to-developers-fearing-ai-slop-and-clones), which first reported the session.

The headline line came from Giardino: "We don't want to be gatekeepers telling developers which tools they should or shouldn't use." Valve did not announce any new AI policy, and [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/software/ai/valve-admits-steam-has-an-ai-slop-and-cloning-problem-but-says-we-dont-want-to-be-gatekeepers-telling-developers-which-tools-they-should-or-shouldnt-use/) noted that Valve does not appear to be planning major changes to developer access any time soon.

Valve did not deny the problem, though. Wilkinson told the room, "We are fully aware of the situation, and a significant number of people are thinking about how to solve this problem." Giardino added that "there's plenty of room for improvement" and described Valve's goal as making Steam "a smarter and more dynamic platform."

## The AI clone extortion emails

The most alarming part of the session was a developer describing a new threat. Indie studios have been receiving emails that say, in effect, "If you don't pay, we'll clone your game with AI and release it first," according to Inven Global's account.

[TweakTown](https://www.tweaktown.com/news/114349/valve-responds-to-steam-devs-getting-extortion-emails-threatening-ai-clones-of-their-games/index.html) reported that one indie developer had previously received an email demanding $200 in Steam items within 24 hours in exchange for not releasing a clone of their game.

Giardino said it was the first time he had heard of such cases. Valve's position is that extortion is closer to a law enforcement issue than something a game platform can solve on its own, but the company still asked developers to report every case to Steam so it can investigate and help.

If you are a developer and you get one of these emails:

- **Do not pay.** Paying signals that the tactic works.
- **Keep the evidence.** Save the email, headers, sender account names and any Steam profile links.
- **Report it to Steam,** as Valve asked developers to do in Busan.
- **Report it to the police** or your national cybercrime unit, since Valve itself framed this as a law enforcement matter.

## Why cloning got faster with AI

Copycat games are not new. Valve's own framing, as reported by Inven Global, is that developers have long spotted an interesting idea during Steam Next Fest and quickly shipped something similar. What has changed is speed: AI-powered tools have shortened the time it takes for a clone to reach a playable state and compete with the original.

Valve's advice is the same philosophy it applies to piracy. It has historically not leaned on aggressive DRM, and instead tries to make the legitimate version the best one available. For AI clones, that means building a community, running a developer website, talking to players and shipping steady updates, because a growing game with its own identity is harder to copy than a single mechanic. Valve also acknowledged that community alone is not enough, since it takes time to build.

### Studios are already pulling demos

Some developers are not waiting. Digital Sun Games, the studio behind Moonlighter, had planned a public playtest of its "Tower Defensevania" ReVamp, but scaled it back to a "more contained" test on its Discord server starting October 29, [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/programming/moonlighter-developer-delays-public-playtest-of-next-game-over-concerns-of-ai-cloning) reported. The studio said it was "concerned by recent news of games being reverse-engineered, cloned, and published before the original is even out" and that "with launch still months away, a large public build of ReVamp felt like too big a risk."

Digital Sun also wrote, "Showing games early is how indies get seen and find the fans who keep us going," and added, "We hope platforms keep strengthening protections for original work." The same report noted that Alliance Arts and Who's You cancelled a Next Fest demo for Drunken Goddess Reflux over similar fears.

According to PC Gamer, Digital Sun's Israel Mallén has suggested Steam give developers tools to vet who gets access to early builds, using signals such as "account age, playtime or review history."

## How many Steam AI games are there?

The numbers explain why the topic keeps trending. Steam's AI disclosure box rolled out in January 2024. A census of roughly 53,600 Steam games released between mid-2023 and mid-2026, run by Sulka Haro, co-founder of the studio behind Pax Dei, found the share of new games carrying an AI disclosure has climbed every year, as reported by [PC Gamer in July](https://www.pcgamer.com/gaming-industry/steam-week-in-review-take-cover-because-it-looks-like-more-than-half-of-steam-games-will-have-an-ai-disclosure-by-2027-2028/).

| Year | Share of new Steam games with an AI disclosure |
| --- | --- |
| 2024 | 10.9% |
| 2025 | 19.9% |
| 2026 (through July) | 30.8% |

Haro found AI-flagged launches rose from about 13 a month before the disclosure requirement to about 530 a month, while non-AI launches grew only modestly, from about 1,030 to about 1,320 a month. If that trend holds, around half of new Steam games could carry an AI disclosure sometime in 2027 or 2028. Haro's data also suggests AI-flagged games rarely earn much money and are seldom reviewed well by players.

Overall volume is huge too. PC Gamer cites a SteamDB chart showing nearly 23,000 games released on Steam in 2026 so far, before November has even started. And at the June 2026 Next Fest, [Gfinity](https://www.gfinityesports.com/article/ai-infestation-is-sapping-my-excitement-for-steam-next-fest) counted more than 8,600 participating games, with nearly 1,700 disclosing some form of generative AI use. All of these figures rely on developers reporting honestly, so the real totals may be higher.

## Next Fest and the AI slop problem

Steam Next Fest is where the friction is most visible. The October 2026 edition ran through October 26 at 10 a.m. Pacific, per the Steam Team's announcement quoted by [Out of Games](https://outof.games/news/10171-steam-next-fest-october-2026-has-started-play-new-game-demos-until-october-26/), with "thousands of FREE demos."

Wilkinson explained why low-effort games still show up for players. During the first two days of Next Fest, Steam shows games almost at random so every entry gets a chance, then switches to personalized recommendations. "We recognize that the trade-off is some low-quality games get exposed," he said. That random window is a lifeline for small studios without a marketing budget, but it is also an opening for mass-produced AI entries.

## What it means for players and developers

For **players**, nothing changes overnight. AI-made games will keep appearing, and the best tool you have is the "AI Generated Content Disclosure" section near the bottom of each store page. Valve rewrote those rules in January 2026, according to [VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/valve-has-significantly-rewritten-steams-rules-for-how-developers-much-disclose-ai-use). Disclosure now covers two cases: AI used to generate content for the game, its store page or marketing, and AI content generated live during gameplay. AI coding helpers and similar developer tools do not need to be disclosed, so a disclosure tells you about the art, audio and text you will see, not every tool used behind the scenes.

For **developers**, the message from Valve is to compete on identity and updates, report threats, and expect Steam's discovery systems, not new bans, to do the filtering. The open question is whether Valve adds the kind of early-access vetting tools Mallén described.
