> **The short version:** The France protests began in September with high school students complaining about missing teachers and have grown into nationwide unrest driven by anger over the cost of living. After riots and looting on October 17 and 18, President Emmanuel Macron is allowing only union marches this weekend, 70,000 police are deployed, and the Louvre is closed. Talks with students and unions start Monday.

The France protests are one of the most searched world stories today, as a second big weekend of demonstrations begins under new nationwide restrictions on who may march. Paris has shut its major museums, cities from Lyon to Marseille have banned or limited rallies, and the government says it will be "uncompromising" with anyone who turns violent. Below is how the movement started, what happened last weekend, what the new bans actually cover, and what to watch in the days ahead.

## How the France protests started

The movement did not begin as a national revolt. According to [AP](https://apnews.com/article/france-protests-paris-unrest-ad2fb9206e72e5d1b4ae43fdf7aa96a9), it first erupted at a high school southeast of Paris over a lack of staff. [RFI](https://www.rfi.fr/en/france/20261021-what-we-know-about-the-people-behind-france-s-riots-and-looting) dates the launch to mid-September, when high school pupils began demonstrating over failings in education.

Their early demands were specific:

- More staff and better funding for schools, especially in lower-income neighborhoods (AP).
- For some students, reform of the university admissions system (AP).
- Respect and a real hearing from the government. "I feel we are not respected as young adults," Jade, a 19-year-old medical student who told the BBC she lives on €300 a month, said in Limoges.

Over several weeks, the student protests merged with a broader anger. Al Jazeera describes a nationwide call to mobilize that brought together former Yellow Vest activists, secondary school and university students, climate activists and even farmers. [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/europe/frances-macron-tightens-curbs-protests-blaming-hard-left-unrest-2026-10-22/) says the unrest has been fueled by soaring energy costs, pressure on household budgets and concerns over deteriorating public services.

The BBC sums up the backdrop: one of the biggest fiscal deficits in Europe, a presidential election six months away, and a centrist government short on solutions, on a majority in the National Assembly, and on time.

## What happened on October 17 and 18

The turning point was the nationwide day of action on Saturday, October 17. Al Jazeera reports that more than 500,000 people gathered across France. Most marched peacefully, but violence on the fringes shocked the country.

The most powerful image came from Limoges, in central France, where the 19th-century city hall was set on fire and its wooden clock tower collapsed. The [BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cxyvrged14d0o) reports that two men, aged 66 and 34, have been arrested, and that prosecutors say they have links to "radical-left networks."

Reaction in the city is split. "It is a historical tragedy for France, not just Limoges," said Guillaume Guérin. Jean Roger Faucher, 63, who was married in the building, said: "It left us stunned." A 24-year-old named Alexander saw it differently: "Of course it was really sad, it's a symbol of the republic, but what is more important: a town hall or people living in poverty?"

### Who was arrested?

The government has blamed organized far-left groups. "There are delinquents on the one hand, and then there is the planning of an insurrection led by far-left groups," government spokesperson Maud Bregeon said, according to RFI.

Court cases tell a more mixed story. RFI found that about 70% of those taken into custody were adults, most of them men, and the profiles ranged widely: two 18-year-old vocational students with no criminal record in Brest, a 22-year-old in Rennes who held three jobs, and a 43-year-old delivery driver in Pau sentenced to four months in prison. "There is no typical profile," lawyer Sonia Boundaoui told RFI. Paris prosecutor Laure Beccuau told TF1: "I absolutely can't tell you what political position they wish to take."

## Why Macron is banning protests

On Thursday, October 22, Macron said "illegitimate" protests would be banned in the coming days. "Last Saturday we had political and seditious acts by ultra-left groups whose aim is to bring down the Republic," he said, according to Reuters.

The rule he set is simple: demonstrations organized by groups such as trade unions can go ahead, but may be diverted along alternative routes to protect businesses. Others are not authorized. Macron said the bans are needed to protect city centers, businesses and public buildings, AP reports.

Justice Minister Gérald Darmanin went further. Reuters reports he wants voting rights suspended for people who defy protest bans, and restrictions on social media access for people involved in violence, looting or attacks on police. "If we don't deliver an extremely firm penal response, then residents will take justice into their own hands," he said earlier in the week, per RFI.

Macron also hit back at foreign criticism. [DW](https://www.dw.com/en/frances-macron-says-illegitimate-protests-will-be-banned-in-coming-days/a-79735137) reports that he railed against "stupid" comments made by foreign leaders for political gain, after US President Donald Trump weighed in on the unrest.

## What is open, closed and banned this weekend

Here is what [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/23/france-tightens-security-in-anticipation-of-weekend-protests) and AP report for Saturday, October 24:

- **Police:** about 70,000 officers nationwide, roughly 10,000 more than last weekend, with elite units, mounted police, helicopters, drones, armored vehicles and water cannons.
- **Expected turnout:** nearly 200,000 people, including 40,000 in Paris, according to a police source quoted by AFP.
- **Paris:** one union march on a limited route; rallies requested by the student union UNEF and the Yellow Vest movement were rejected. A nighttime curfew is extended until Monday.
- **Lyon:** gatherings banned in central areas, plus curfews and public transport restrictions.
- **Marseille:** protests banned, with one union march allowed "on an exceptional basis."
- **Orleans:** the gathering is barred from the city center. The far-right National Rally is holding its annual meeting there, and about 50 organizations have called a protest against the far right on the sidelines, AP says.
- **Nice and Montpellier:** some rallies banned by local authorities (AP).
- **Culture:** the Louvre, Orsay Museum, Picasso Museum, Grand Palais and Louis Vuitton Foundation are closed. Art Basel Paris ended on Friday, two days early.
- **Shops:** businesses in major cities have been urged to close, and many in central Paris have boarded up windows. Reuters reports Hermès stepped up security at its French stores.

Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez set the tone: "We will be uncompromising with rioters, looters and all those who commit acts of violence." He added, "We are not talking about a nice, friendly demonstration."

## Critics say the bans go too far

Rights groups and unions are pushing back. Amnesty International called the restrictions "a slide toward authoritarian practice" that jeopardizes the right to demonstrate peacefully, according to Al Jazeera. Marylise Léon, head of the CFDT union, said the bans were "counterproductive" given the level of anger. UNEF was blunter: "Macron is trampling upon the freedom to protest in our country, and is spitting on the values of the republic he claims to defend."

Human Rights Watch has also expressed alarm over reports of children severely injured by crowd-control weapons, Al Jazeera reports.

Students say they have been heard but not answered. Macron met high school students for the first time on Friday. Nathanael Combary, president of the campaign group High School Renewal, said Macron had been "attentive," but added: "We are not satisfied," and urged pupils to "continue to mobilise."

## Why the France protests matter beyond France

This is not only a domestic story. Reuters reports that on Thursday the risk premium on French government debt over German bonds widened to its highest in 15 years, France's benchmark 10-year yield hit 5.14%, its highest since July 2002, and French yields topped US Treasuries for the first time since 2013. Higher borrowing costs make it harder for any government to fund the school spending and cost of living relief protesters are demanding.

Politics is the other pressure point. France votes for Macron's successor next spring, and the BBC quotes Guérin warning that "what happened here will have an influence on the upcoming presidential election."
